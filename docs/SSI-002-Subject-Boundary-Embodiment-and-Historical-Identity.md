## Abstract

Subjective Structural Intelligence (SSI) begins with a question that evaluation-centered architectures cannot answer by scoring alone:

**Whose choice is it?**

Before an intelligent system can meaningfully be described as perceiving, evaluating, choosing, or evolving from a particular perspective, its subject-relative organization must be investigated.

This paper examines four foundational dimensions:

1. Subject formation.
2. Subject boundaries.
3. Embodiment and situated capabilities.
4. Historical identity and continuity.

We argue that none of these dimensions can be reduced to ownership, computational enclosure, state representation, or isolated decision-making.

A computational process may have a clear execution boundary without possessing meaningful agency. A system may be owned by one organization while representing the interests of another. Two systems may share identical computational states without sharing the same causal history. A system may preserve its historical identity while undergoing substantial structural change.

We propose a candidate model of functional subjecthood based on boundary coherence, situated interaction, historical continuity, evaluative integration, and action attribution.

We further introduce the concept of a **Historical Identity Graph (HIG)**: a proposed structural representation of the causal and authorized transformations through which a subject's state, commitments, capabilities, and evaluative organization evolve.

The HIG is intended as an operational research construct, not as a solution to philosophical personal identity or phenomenal consciousness.

The paper distinguishes subject boundaries from ownership boundaries, biological embodiment from computational situatedness, and state similarity from historical continuity.

It also identifies candidate mechanisms through which Structural Intelligence components—including MDT, UTN, CCC, Calling Graphs, and Scoring Forests—could support subject-relative organization.

The resulting framework provides a foundation for later SSI research on perception, valuation, agency, self-modeling, and worldview evolution.

**Keywords:** Subjective Structural Intelligence, Subjecthood, Subject Boundary, Embodiment, Situated Intelligence, Historical Identity, Causal Continuity, Agency, Structural Identity, Historical Identity Graph, Scoring Forest, Structural Intelligence

# 1. Introduction: Before Intelligence Chooses, Who Is Choosing?

## 1.1 The Missing Subject Problem

A modern intelligent system may contain:

- A perception pipeline.
- A memory subsystem.
- A world model.
- A Scoring Forest.
- A policy engine.
- A decision mechanism.
- An action executor.
- A learning mechanism.

Together, these components can support sophisticated behavior.

But their existence does not automatically establish a coherent subject.

A collection of computational modules can process information and generate decisions without representing a unified perspective.

Likewise, an organization can operate a collection of intelligent systems without those systems constituting a single computational subject.

The question is therefore not simply:

> Does the system perform intelligent computations?

It is:

> Under what conditions can these computations be attributed to a coherent, historically situated, action-capable subject?

SSI-001 introduced this question as part of the transition from Structural Intelligence to Subjective Structural Intelligence.

SSI-002 examines its structural foundations.

## 1.2 Why the Question Matters

Consider an AI assistant operating on behalf of a human.

The assistant may:

- Maintain memory.
- Infer preferences.
- Evaluate alternatives.
- Execute authorized actions.
- Learn from feedback.

But several entities remain distinguishable:

1. The human whose interests may be represented.
2. The organization operating the AI service.
3. The computational system performing the evaluation.
4. The authority responsible for action permissions.
5. Other people affected by the resulting actions.

These entities may have overlapping but nonidentical interests and boundaries.

If they are collapsed into a single abstract "agent," important distinctions disappear.

SSI therefore treats subject attribution as an architectural problem requiring explicit analysis.

## 1.3 The Central Thesis

We propose:

**Functional subjecthood is not established by a boundary alone, but may emerge as a useful structural characterization when boundary coherence, situated interaction, historical continuity, evaluative integration, and attributable action are jointly organized.**

This is a candidate research thesis.

It is not a proven necessary-and-sufficient definition of subjecthood.

Nor does it imply that a system satisfying these properties possesses phenomenal consciousness.

The proposed framework is intended to support operational modeling, comparative experiments, and falsifiable hypotheses.

# 2. What Is a Subject?

## 2.1 Subject as an Organized Center of Interaction

A subject is often described informally as an entity that experiences, perceives, or acts.

However, these terms have different meanings across philosophy, biology, psychology, and computer science.

SSI therefore introduces a restricted operational concept:

**A functional structural subject is a candidate system whose information processing, evaluation, action, and adaptation can be coherently attributed to a distinguishable, situated, historically connected organization.**

This definition emphasizes attribution rather than consciousness.

It asks whether a system's behavior can be understood through an identifiable organization of:

- Boundaries.
- Capabilities.
- Memory.
- Evaluators.
- Commitments.
- Actions.
- Feedback.
- Structural transformations.

It does not assume that every computational system meeting some of these conditions is a subject.

## 2.2 Subjecthood Is Not Binary by Assumption

Subjecthood may be investigated through multiple operational dimensions.

A candidate vector is:

\[
\mathbf{Q}_S =
(q_B,q_E,q_H,q_V,q_A)
\]

where:

- \(q_B\): boundary coherence.
- \(q_E\): situated interaction capability.
- \(q_H\): historical continuity.
- \(q_V\): evaluative integration.
- \(q_A\): action attribution and regulation.

These quantities are proposed measurement dimensions, not established universal metrics.

A system might demonstrate strong boundary coherence but weak agency.

Another might demonstrate sophisticated evaluation but little persistent historical continuity.

A third might possess extensive memory but lack authority over consequential actions.

Such differences suggest that functional subjecthood should initially be studied as a multidimensional research construct.

No universal scalar subjecthood score is assumed.

## 2.3 The Subject Is Not a Scoring Function

Suppose a system evaluates alternatives using:

\[
V(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i f_i(a)
\]

where:

- \(a\): candidate alternative.
- \(f_i\): evaluation function.
- \(w_i\): evaluation weight.

This may be a useful computational model.

But it does not explain:

- Who selected the evaluators.
- Whose interests the weights represent.
- Who bears the consequences.
- Who can challenge the scoring criteria.
- Whether the evaluative structure persists through time.
- Whether its changes belong to a coherent history.

A subject cannot therefore be identified simply with a scoring function.

**Subject ≠ Scoring Function.**

This distinction remains essential even when the scoring function is implemented through a sophisticated Scoring Forest.

## 2.4 The Subject Is Not the Owner

Ownership and subjecthood concern different relationships.

Ownership may be established through legal, contractual, or institutional arrangements.

Functional subjecthood concerns the organization of perception, evaluation, action, and historical continuity.

An AI system may be owned by an organization while modeling a human user's preferences.

A human may authorize an AI system to perform tasks without transferring personal identity or unrestricted decision authority.

A distributed system may be jointly operated by multiple organizations without possessing a unified evaluative perspective.

Therefore:

**Ownership may constrain a subject's operational environment, but ownership alone does not establish subject identity.**

# 3. Subject Boundaries: Necessary Organization, Insufficient Evidence

## 3.1 The Boundary Problem

Every computational system operates within some boundaries.

Examples include:

- Process boundaries.
- Memory boundaries.
- Network boundaries.
- Security boundaries.
- Resource boundaries.
- Organizational boundaries.
- Policy boundaries.

But boundaries serve different purposes.

A database partition separates information.

A sandbox restricts execution.

A legal boundary establishes institutional responsibility.

A biological membrane separates and regulates material exchange.

These boundaries should not be treated as equivalent.

The presence of a boundary is insufficient to establish a subject.

## 3.2 A Multidimensional Boundary Model

We propose representing subject boundaries as:

\[
B_S =
(B_P,B_I,B_R,B_A,B_G)
\]

where:

- \(B_P\): physical or execution boundary.
- \(B_I\): information and memory boundary.
- \(B_R\): resource and capability boundary.
- \(B_A\): action-authority boundary.
- \(B_G\): governance and policy boundary.

The components may overlap.

They may also change independently.

For example, a software agent may receive additional memory access without receiving additional execution permissions.

A robot may retain the same physical body while losing authority to operate certain actuators.

A human may retain personal identity despite changes in institutional responsibilities.

These examples suggest that boundary evolution and identity evolution are related but not identical.

## 3.3 Boundary Coherence

A boundary is coherent when its relevant components support a consistent operational organization.

Potential properties include:

1. State attribution.
2. Resource attribution.
3. Action attribution.
4. Controlled information exchange.
5. Persistent operational constraints.
6. Traceable boundary changes.

We propose:

\[
C_B(S,t)=
\mathcal{C}(B_S(t),I_S(t),A_S(t))
\]

where:

- \(C_B\): candidate boundary-coherence measure.
- \(B_S\): boundary structure.
- \(I_S\): internal state relationships.
- \(A_S\): action-attribution relationships.

The function \(\mathcal{C}\) remains to be operationally defined.

It should not be interpreted as an established measure of consciousness or personhood.

## 3.4 Boundary Permeability

Subject boundaries are rarely completely closed.

A biological organism exchanges matter and information with its environment.

A computational agent exchanges messages, observations, and tool outputs.

A human subject participates in social and institutional relationships.

Therefore, subject boundaries should be understood as selectively permeable.

A candidate interaction model is:

\[
X_{\mathrm{in}}
\xrightarrow{B_S}
S
\xrightarrow{B_S}
A_{\mathrm{out}}
\]

The boundary mediates incoming information and outgoing action.

It may also regulate:

- Memory import.
- Knowledge transfer.
- Evaluator installation.
- Policy updates.
- Delegated execution.
- Structural self-modification.

This creates a direct connection between SSI and the Structural Policy Control Plane.

## 3.5 Boundary Change Is Not Automatically Identity Change

A subject may acquire a new capability without becoming a different subject.

A robot may gain a new sensor.

A human may use a prosthetic device.

An AI agent may receive a new tool.

These changes modify operational boundaries.

They do not necessarily destroy historical continuity.

Conversely, a system may retain the same external interface while undergoing complete internal state replacement.

Thus:

**Boundary similarity is neither sufficient nor always necessary for operational identity continuity.**

# 4. Embodiment: Intelligence Situated in a World

## 4.1 Biological Embodiment

Biological embodiment involves physical processes that support perception, action, regulation, and survival.

These include:

- Sensory systems.
- Motor systems.
- Metabolism.
- Homeostasis.
- Physical vulnerability.
- Spatial location.
- Temporal persistence.

Human cognition is deeply shaped by bodily capacities and limitations.

SSI recognizes this connection without assuming that every form of functional subjecthood must reproduce biological embodiment.

## 4.2 Computational and Operational Embodiment

An artificial system may possess operationally relevant forms of situated interaction.

Examples include:

- Sensor interfaces.
- Actuator interfaces.
- Persistent execution state.
- Limited computational resources.
- Network dependencies.
- Tool permissions.
- Environmental feedback.

We propose distinguishing:

**Biological embodiment:** organismic physical organization.

**Robotic embodiment:** physical sensorimotor interaction through engineered hardware.

**Operational situatedness:** computational interaction constrained by interfaces, resources, permissions, and persistent state.

These categories are not interchangeable.

A language-model-based agent with tool access should not automatically be described as biologically embodied.

Nevertheless, operational situatedness may influence its decisions in structurally important ways.

## 4.3 Affordances and Subject-Relative Action Possibilities

An environment offers different action possibilities to different subjects.

A door may be:

- Easily opened by an adult.
- Difficult for a small child.
- Inaccessible to a robot lacking a manipulator.
- Accessible to a software-controlled building system through an authorized actuator.

The object remains the same.

The available actions differ.

A candidate affordance model is:

\[
\mathcal{A}(x,S_t)
=
f(x,E_t,B_t,R_t)
\]

where:

- \(x\): environmental object or situation.
- \(S_t\): subject state.
- \(E_t\): embodied or operational capabilities.
- \(B_t\): relevant boundaries.
- \(R_t\): available resources.

This formulation suggests that practical meaning depends partly on the subject's capabilities.

## 4.4 Embodiment Changes Evaluation

Suppose two agents observe the same obstacle.

One can climb.

The other cannot.

Their evaluations may differ even if they share the same general objective.

A candidate model is:

\[
V(a,S_t)=
\Psi(a,P_t,E_t,R_t,G_t)
\]

where:

- \(P_t\): perceptual representation.
- \(E_t\): capabilities.
- \(R_t\): resources.
- \(G_t\): goals and commitments.

Embodiment is therefore not merely an input modality.

It may influence the meaning, feasibility, risk, and value of available actions.

This relationship is central to SSI's concept of situated evaluation.

## 4.5 Embodiment and the Scoring Forest

A Scoring Forest can evaluate candidate actions.

But an embodiment-aware Scoring Forest must account for the subject's actual capabilities.

An action that scores highly in the abstract may be infeasible for the subject.

We therefore propose separating:

1. Abstract alternative evaluation.
2. Subject-specific feasibility.
3. Subject-specific consequences.
4. Authority and policy constraints.
5. Final action selection.

This separation can prevent a scoring system from treating unavailable or unauthorized actions as legitimate choices.

# 5. Historical Identity: The Subject Is More Than Its Present State

## 5.1 The Snapshot Problem

Consider two computational systems with identical current memory and parameters.

Their present states may be indistinguishable.

But suppose they arrived at those states through different histories.

System A developed through a long sequence of interactions and authorized updates.

System B was created by copying A's state.

Are they the same subject?

State equality alone cannot settle the question.

This motivates the distinction:

**State Similarity ≠ Historical Identity.**

## 5.2 Historical Identity as Causal Continuity

SSI proposes investigating operational identity through causal and historical relationships.

A subject's history may include:

- State transitions.
- Experiences.
- Decisions.
- Actions.
- Feedback.
- Commitments.
- Evaluator revisions.
- Capability changes.
- Boundary changes.
- Authorized structural transformations.

We propose representing a subject's history as:

\[
H_S(t)=
\{e_0,e_1,\ldots,e_t\}
\]

where each \(e_i\) is a historically relevant event or transformation.

However, a simple event list is insufficient.

Historical identity also depends on relationships among events.

This motivates a graph-based representation.

## 5.3 The Historical Identity Graph

We introduce the **Historical Identity Graph (HIG)** as a candidate SSI construct.

\[
HIG_S=(N_H,E_H,\Lambda_H)
\]

where:

- \(N_H\): historically relevant states and events.
- \(E_H\): causal and transformation relationships.
- \(\Lambda_H\): metadata describing provenance, authorization, commitments, and continuity conditions.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Initial Subject State
         |
         v
Experience E1
         |
         v
Decision D1
         |
         v
Action A1
         |
         v
Feedback F1
         |
         v
Evaluator Revision R1
         |
         v
Updated Subject State
         |
         v
New Experience E2
         |
         v
Further Structural Evolution
```

**Figure reference:** `figures/Fig-002-Subject-Boundary-Embodiment-and-Historical-Identity.png` (planned)

The HIG is not intended to store every computational operation.

It should represent transformations relevant to the chosen operational identity criteria.

## 5.4 Why a Graph Rather Than a Timeline?

A timeline records sequence.

A graph can additionally represent:

- Branching.
- Merging.
- Delegation.
- Copying.
- Shared influences.
- Conflicting commitments.
- Structural inheritance.
- Alternative continuity claims.

For example, an AI system may create two copies from a shared checkpoint.

Both copies inherit a common history.

After divergence, each develops a different sequence of experiences and actions.

The resulting structure is not a single linear identity chain.

It is a branching history.

This distinction is especially important for artificial systems because copying and branching are ordinary computational operations.

## 5.5 Identity Continuity Is Not Automatically Unique

Suppose:

\[
S_0 \rightarrow S_1
\]

and then:

\[
S_1 \rightarrow
\begin{cases}
S_{2a}\\
S_{2b}
\end{cases}
\]

Both successors may preserve:

- Memory.
- Historical provenance.
- Core commitments.
- Structural organization.

Yet they subsequently develop independent histories.

SSI does not assume that both successors are numerically identical to the original subject in the philosophical sense.

Instead, it proposes distinguishing:

- Shared ancestry.
- Operational continuity.
- Branch-specific identity.
- Legal or institutional identity.
- Phenomenal identity, if applicable.

These are different questions.

The HIG can represent shared ancestry and branch divergence without claiming to resolve all forms of personal identity.

# 6. Identity Through Structural Change

## 6.1 Persistence Does Not Require Immutability

A subject may change while retaining meaningful continuity.

Examples include:

- Learning new knowledge.
- Revising beliefs.
- Acquiring capabilities.
- Changing goals.
- Correcting evaluators.
- Reorganizing memories.
- Adopting new commitments.

Therefore, identity continuity cannot require complete structural immutability.

We propose:

\[
S_{t+1}=
\mathcal{T}(S_t,\Delta_t)
\]

where:

- \(S_t\): subject state.
- \(\Delta_t\): structural change.
- \(\mathcal{T}\): transformation process.

The research question becomes:

**Which transformations preserve which dimensions of identity continuity?**

## 6.2 Four Forms of Continuity

We propose distinguishing four candidate continuity dimensions.

### 6.2.1 Causal Continuity

The new state is causally connected to the prior state.

### 6.2.2 Historical Continuity

Relevant memories, experiences, and transformation records remain connected.

### 6.2.3 Commitment Continuity

Selected values, responsibilities, and commitments persist or are revised through recognized procedures.

### 6.2.4 Agency Continuity

The system retains or appropriately transforms the organization through which actions are selected and attributed.

These dimensions may diverge.

A system may preserve memory while losing action authority.

Another may preserve execution continuity while replacing its evaluative commitments.

A third may retain institutional identity despite substantial personnel or infrastructure changes.

SSI should not collapse these differences into a single unexplained identity score.

## 6.3 A Candidate Continuity Vector

We propose:

\[
\mathbf{I}_{t,t+1}=
(I_C,I_H,I_K,I_A)
\]

where:

- \(I_C\): causal continuity.
- \(I_H\): historical continuity.
- \(I_K\): commitment continuity.
- \(I_A\): agency continuity.

The vector is intended to support comparative analysis.

Its components require operational definitions and validation.

It does not establish numerical personal identity or phenomenal continuity.

## 6.4 Authorized Transformation

Authorization is relevant to governance and accountability.

However, authorization alone does not determine identity.

An unauthorized update may preserve causal continuity while violating policy.

An authorized replacement may preserve institutional continuity while interrupting computational continuity.

Therefore:

**Authorization and identity continuity must be modeled as separate dimensions.**

A Historical Identity Graph should record both.

This separation is essential for distinguishing:

- What happened.
- Why it happened.
- Who authorized it.
- Which continuity properties were preserved.
- Which responsibilities remain attributable.

## 7.1 Four Interdependent Foundations

SSI-002 proposes four foundational structures:

1. Subject organization.
2. Boundary organization.
3. Embodied or operational situatedness.
4. Historical continuity.

These dimensions are mutually related.

Boundaries regulate interaction.

Embodiment determines available capabilities.

Historical experience influences interpretation and evaluation.

The resulting organization supports candidate forms of subject-relative action.

## 7.2 The Subject-State Model

Building on SSI-001, we propose:

\[
S_t=
(B_t,E_t,H_t,V_t,A_t,M_t)
\]

where:

- \(B_t\): boundary organization.
- \(E_t\): embodiment or operational situatedness.
- \(H_t\): historical identity structure.
- \(V_t\): evaluative organization.
- \(A_t\): agency organization.
- \(M_t\): self-model.

Perception and worldview, developed in later SSI papers, interact with these components.

The model is intentionally modular.

A future implementation may use different internal representations.

The important research objective is to preserve the distinctions among boundary, capability, history, evaluation, agency, and self-modeling.

## 7.3 Historical Identity as a Constraint on Structural Growth

Structural growth changes the organization of intelligence.

But unconstrained growth can undermine:

- Commitments.
- Evaluator provenance.
- Responsibility attribution.
- Historical traceability.
- Authorized decision boundaries.

SSI proposes that important structural transformations should be evaluated against selected continuity requirements.

A candidate condition is:

\[
\operatorname{Accept}(\Delta_t)
=
\operatorname{Valid}(\Delta_t)
\land
\operatorname{Authorized}(\Delta_t)
\land
\operatorname{ContinuityAcceptable}(\Delta_t)
\]

This condition is intended for governed structural changes.

It is not a universal law of biological or psychological identity.

The three predicates require explicit policy and experimental definitions.

A transformation may be technically valid but unauthorized.

It may be authorized but destructive to selected continuity properties.

It may preserve continuity while producing poor task performance.

These outcomes must be distinguished.

# 8. Subject Boundaries and the Policy Control Plane

## 8.1 Boundary Is Not Policy

A boundary defines a distinction or interface.

A policy determines permitted behavior.

The two are related but different.

A subject may have the technical capability to perform an action while lacking permission to execute it.

Similarly, a system may be authorized to perform an action that its current hardware cannot support.

We therefore distinguish:

\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feasible}}
\]

from:

\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{authorized}}
\]

The executable action set is constrained by both:

\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{executable}}
=
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feasible}}
\cap
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{authorized}}
\]

This formulation assumes that the two sets are defined over a common action space.

## 8.2 Evaluation Authority and Action Authority

An intelligent system may be permitted to evaluate an action without being permitted to execute it.

It may also be permitted to propose changes to its evaluators without being permitted to install those changes.

SSI therefore distinguishes:

- Perception authority.
- Information-access authority.
- Evaluation authority.
- Decision authority.
- Execution authority.
- Structural-revision authority.

These permissions need not belong to the same entity.

This is especially important in human-AI collaboration.

A human may retain final decision authority while delegating computational evaluation.

## 8.3 The Subject-Authority Relationship

We propose representing authority relationships explicitly:

\[
\mathcal{R}_{SA}
=
\{(S_i,a_j,\pi_k)\}
\]

where:

- \(S_i\): subject or represented stakeholder.
- \(a_j\): action or structural operation.
- \(\pi_k\): applicable authorization rule.

The relationship should distinguish:

1. The subject whose interests are represented.
2. The entity granting authorization.
3. The system performing evaluation.
4. The system executing the action.
5. The entity bearing responsibility.

These roles may overlap but should not be assumed identical.

## 8.4 Why This Matters for SSI

Without explicit authority relationships, a system may confuse:

- Representing a user's interests.
- Acting on a user's instructions.
- Choosing on behalf of the user.
- Modifying the user's inferred preferences.
- Replacing the user's evaluative commitments.

SSI treats these as distinct operations.

The distinction provides a foundation for later research on human-AI evaluation sovereignty.

# 9. Historical Identity and Self-Models

## 9.1 A Self-Model Is Not a History

A self-model represents information about the current or anticipated subject.

A Historical Identity Graph represents relevant causal and transformation relationships.

The two structures interact but are not identical.

A self-model may contain:

- Current capabilities.
- Current goals.
- Current limitations.
- Current commitments.
- Estimated competence.
- Expected future behavior.

A historical model may contain:

- Prior states.
- Past actions.
- Evaluator revisions.
- Commitment changes.
- Feedback.
- Provenance.
- Branching and copying events.

The distinction can be summarized:

**Self-Model: What am I represented as now?**

**Historical Identity: How did this organization arrive here?**

## 9.2 Historical Evidence for Self-Model Revision

A system may revise its self-model using historical evidence.

For example:

- Repeated failures may reduce estimated competence.
- Successful adaptation may increase confidence in a capability.
- Changes in authorization may alter available actions.
- Contradictory outcomes may trigger evaluator revision.

A candidate model is:

\[
M_{t+1}
=
\mathcal{U}_M(M_t,HIG_t,F_t)
\]

where:

- \(M_t\): current self-model.
- \(HIG_t\): historical identity structure.
- \(F_t\): relevant feedback.

This creates an opportunity for evidence-grounded self-modeling.

It also introduces risks.

A system may selectively interpret its own history.

It may overemphasize successes.

It may erase inconvenient failures.

It may reconstruct its history to justify current preferences.

These risks motivate explicit provenance and counter-evidence mechanisms.

## 9.3 Historical Identity and Self-Referential Scoring

A Scoring Forest may evaluate the subject's current actions.

A reflective evaluator may additionally examine historical consistency.

For example:

- Does the proposed action conflict with a persistent commitment?
- Has the subject changed its evaluation criteria?
- Was that change authorized?
- Does the current self-model match historical performance?
- Has the system ignored important counter-evidence?

These questions suggest a historical meta-evaluation layer.

\[
F_{\mathrm{historical}}
=
\operatorname{Evaluate}(HIG_t,M_t,V_t)
\]

The output need not be a scalar score.

It may consist of:

- Consistency findings.
- Provenance reports.
- Conflict flags.
- Uncertainty estimates.
- Revision recommendations.

This provides a potential interface between SSI and UCPSF.

# 10. Three Structural Identity Thought Experiments

## 10.1 Experiment A: Identical State, Different History

Consider two agents:

**Agent A**

- Learns through repeated interaction.
- Accumulates feedback.
- Revises its evaluators.
- Develops persistent commitments.

**Agent B**

- Is initialized from a snapshot of Agent A.
- Receives the same current memory and evaluator state.
- Has not independently undergone Agent A's earlier interactions.

At initialization, the two agents may produce identical outputs.

However, their causal histories differ.

The experiment asks:

1. Which operational properties are identical?
2. Which historical relationships differ?
3. Which identity claims require historical evidence?
4. What happens after the agents encounter different environments?

The key hypothesis is not that the two agents must immediately behave differently.

Rather:

**Behavioral equivalence at a particular moment does not establish identical causal history.**

This is a structural distinction that can be investigated without making claims about phenomenal identity.

## 10.2 Experiment B: One History, Divergent Futures

Consider an agent copied into two branches.

Both branches inherit the same checkpoint.

After copying:

- Branch A receives one set of experiences.
- Branch B receives another.
- Their evaluators gradually diverge.
- Their commitments may change.

The Historical Identity Graph records:

```text
                 Shared History
                       |
                       v
                 Checkpoint S1
                       |
              +--------+--------+
              |                 |
              v                 v
          Branch A          Branch B
              |                 |
              v                 v
         Experience A      Experience B
              |                 |
              v                 v
         Revision A        Revision B
              |                 |
              v                 v
          State A2          State B2
```

This experiment investigates:

- Shared ancestry.
- Branch-specific continuity.
- Evaluator divergence.
- Commitment preservation.
- Responsibility attribution.

It does not assume that copying creates or transfers consciousness.

## 10.3 Experiment C: Same Boundary, Replaced Identity Structure

Consider a long-running AI service.

Its external interface remains unchanged.

Its domain name, API, and user-facing identity remain stable.

However, its internal memory, evaluators, and persistent state are replaced.

The service boundary remains recognizable.

But important dimensions of computational continuity may be interrupted.

This experiment distinguishes:

- Service identity.
- Institutional identity.
- Computational state continuity.
- Historical provenance.
- Functional subject continuity.

The same interface does not establish the same underlying historical organization.

# 11. Candidate Hypotheses and Experimental Program

## H1 — Boundary Coherence Hypothesis

**Hypothesis:** Coherent boundaries contribute to stable attribution of state, evaluation, and action.

### Experimental design

Compare agents with:

- Stable memory and action boundaries.
- Periodically reassigned memory.
- Conflicting action permissions.
- Uncontrolled evaluator sharing.

### Measurements

- Action-attribution consistency.
- Memory-provenance accuracy.
- Policy-conflict frequency.
- Recovery after boundary perturbation.

### Falsification condition

Boundary coherence provides no explanatory advantage over simpler architecture variables under predefined test conditions.

---

## H2 — Situated Capability Hypothesis

**Hypothesis:** Differences in operational capabilities systematically alter subject-relative action evaluation.

### Experimental design

Give agents identical goals and observations but different capabilities.

Examples:

- Different tool access.
- Different actuator access.
- Different resource budgets.
- Different execution permissions.

### Measurements

- Feasible-action recognition.
- Decision quality.
- Unexecutable-action frequency.
- Adaptation after capability changes.

### Falsification condition

Capability-aware representations provide no measurable benefit over capability-agnostic evaluation baselines.

---

## H3 — Historical Provenance Hypothesis

**Hypothesis:** Explicit historical provenance improves the auditability of structural changes.

### Experimental design

Compare systems with:

1. No historical record.
2. Linear event logs.
3. Historical Identity Graphs.

### Measurements

- Transformation reconstruction accuracy.
- Responsibility attribution.
- Branch-origin identification.
- Unauthorized-change detection.
- Audit cost.

### Falsification condition

Graph-based provenance fails to outperform simpler logging approaches after controlling for storage, information content, and implementation complexity.

---

## H4 — Continuity-Aware Growth Hypothesis

**Hypothesis:** Continuity-aware structural revision can improve selected stability properties without necessarily preventing adaptation.

### Experimental design

Compare:

- Unrestricted evaluator revision.
- Fixed evaluator structures.
- Continuity-constrained revision.

### Measurements

- Task adaptation.
- Commitment drift.
- Policy violations.
- Historical traceability.
- Recovery after failed updates.

### Falsification condition

Continuity constraints provide no meaningful stability advantage or impose unacceptable adaptation costs under predefined evaluation criteria.

---

## H5 — State Similarity Versus Historical Identity Hypothesis

**Hypothesis:** State similarity and causal-historical continuity are distinct operational properties that should be measured separately.

### Experimental design

Compare:

- Original continuing agents.
- Snapshot copies.
- Reinitialized agents.
- Branching agents.
- Agents reconstructed from partial history.

### Measurements

- State similarity.
- Behavioral similarity.
- Causal provenance.
- Branch divergence.
- Historical reconstruction accuracy.

### Falsification condition

The proposed historical distinctions fail to support any independent explanatory or operational objective beyond state and behavior similarity.

This hypothesis does not attempt to settle numerical or phenomenal identity.

---

## H6 — Subject-Authority Separation Hypothesis

**Hypothesis:** Explicit separation of subject attribution and authorization reduces selected forms of governance ambiguity.

### Experimental design

Compare systems with:

- Unified ownership and action labels.
- Separate subject and authority metadata.
- Explicit provenance and authorization graphs.

### Measurements

- Unauthorized execution frequency.
- Delegation ambiguity.
- Audit accuracy.
- Conflict detection.
- Correct responsibility attribution.

### Falsification condition

Explicit separation provides no measurable governance benefit under controlled scenarios.

# 12. Integration with Structural Intelligence

## 12.1 MDT and Subject Context

Metric Differential Trees may support localized representation of subject-relevant differences.

Potential uses include:

- Capability differences.
- Historical-state differences.
- Context-dependent evaluation.
- Boundary-sensitive localization.

A candidate subject-context representation is:

\[
C_S=
(B_S,E_S,H_S,G_S)
\]

where \(G_S\) represents goals and commitments.

An MDT-based system could use this context to localize relevant evaluators or actions.

This is a proposed integration, not an established implementation.

## 12.2 UTN and Persistent Structural Identity

Universal Tree Nodes may support structured references to:

- Subject components.
- Historical events.
- Evaluator versions.
- Commitment structures.
- Action capabilities.

A potential design direction is to associate persistent structural identifiers with selected nodes.

However, persistent node identifiers do not automatically establish subject identity.

They support traceability.

Identity continuity requires additional interpretation.

## 12.3 CCC and Boundary-Conditioned Behavior

Contextual Control Constructs may express behavior conditioned on:

- Subject state.
- Capability.
- Policy.
- Historical commitments.
- Environmental context.

For example:

```text
IF:
    Subject has capability C
AND:
    Action A is authorized
AND:
    Historical commitment K is satisfied

THEN:
    Permit candidate action A

ELSE:
    Reject, revise, or escalate
```

This illustrates how subject-relative conditions could be made explicit.

## 12.4 Calling Graphs and Historical Transformation

Calling Graphs may represent:

- Action sequences.
- Delegated operations.
- Evaluator revision procedures.
- Structural transformation dependencies.

A Historical Identity Graph and a Calling Graph serve different purposes.

A Calling Graph may describe execution relationships.

A Historical Identity Graph records identity-relevant historical transformations and their provenance.

The two may be linked through shared event identifiers.

## 12.5 Scoring Forests and Historical Identity

A Scoring Forest may evaluate alternatives using subject-relevant criteria.

A Historical Identity Graph may provide evidence concerning:

- Prior decisions.
- Commitment changes.
- Evaluator reliability.
- Historical consequences.
- Authorization records.

The Scoring Forest can consume selected historical evidence.

But the HIG should not be reduced to a score.

Its primary purpose is to preserve relationships and provenance.

# 13. SSI and Collective Structural Learning

## 13.1 Shared Structures Do Not Imply Shared Identity

Collective Structural Learning enables multiple intelligent systems to exchange reusable structures.

These may include:

- Principles.
- Evaluators.
- Calling Graphs.
- CCC structures.
- PIRP/PIRU units.
- Contextual knowledge.

But structure sharing is not identity sharing.

Two agents may use the same evaluator while retaining distinct:

- Histories.
- Boundaries.
- Commitments.
- Capabilities.
- Action authorities.

Therefore:

**Collective Intelligence ≠ Collective Subject Identity.**

This distinction is particularly important for distributed AI ecosystems.

## 13.2 Structural Inheritance and Localization

A subject may import a reusable intelligence structure.

However, the imported structure may require localization.

For example, an evaluator developed for one robot may assume capabilities unavailable to another robot.

A policy developed for one institution may be unauthorized in another.

A preference model learned from one person should not automatically become authoritative for another person.

SSI therefore proposes that structural transfer should preserve metadata concerning:

- Origin.
- Intended context.
- Capability assumptions.
- Authority requirements.
- Validation evidence.
- Revision history.

## 13.3 The Identity Boundary of Collective Learning

Collective learning can improve individual subjects without merging their identities.

A useful distinction is:

```text
Subject A --------+
                  |
                  v
          Shared Intelligence Pool
                  ^
                  |
Subject B --------+
                  |
                  v
          Localized Adaptation
                  |
          +-------+-------+
          |               |
          v               v
      Subject A'      Subject B'
```

The subjects may learn from shared structures while maintaining distinct historical trajectories.

This provides a possible bridge between SSI and the Portable Intelligence Runtime Pieces and Units research program.

# 14. Philosophical and Scientific Boundaries

## 14.1 Functional Subjecthood Is Not Phenomenal Consciousness

The proposed subject model concerns observable and computationally representable properties.

It does not establish subjective experience.

A system may possess:

- A persistent boundary.
- A historical record.
- A self-model.
- An action-selection mechanism.
- Reflective evaluation.

These properties do not prove that the system experiences anything.

SSI preserves this distinction explicitly.

## 14.2 Historical Identity Is Not Numerical Personal Identity

A Historical Identity Graph can represent causal relationships and transformation provenance.

It cannot, by itself, resolve philosophical questions about whether a copied or transformed system remains numerically the same person.

Those questions involve additional assumptions about identity.

SSI therefore limits its initial claims to operational continuity and attribution.

## 14.3 Embodiment Is Not Equivalent Across Substrates

Biological organisms, robots, and software agents possess different forms of interaction and constraint.

Their structural similarities should not erase their physical and functional differences.

Comparative research must specify which properties are being compared.

## 14.4 Coherence Is Not Moral Legitimacy

A coherent subject may pursue harmful goals.

A stable identity may preserve mistaken beliefs.

A historically continuous organization may exercise illegitimate authority.

Therefore:

**Subject Coherence ≠ Truth ≠ Moral Legitimacy.**

SSI requires independent mechanisms for evidence evaluation, safety, rights, and governance.

# 18. Conclusion: A Subject Is a History, Not Merely a Snapshot

SSI-002 begins with a foundational question:

**What makes intelligent evaluation and action attributable to a particular subject?**

The paper argues that no single computational property is sufficient.

A boundary alone does not establish subjecthood.

Ownership does not establish subject identity.

Embodiment cannot be reduced to biological form, but neither can computational situatedness be assumed equivalent to biological embodiment.

A self-model is not the same as a historical identity.

And two systems with identical current states do not necessarily share the same causal history.

The proposed SSI framework therefore investigates functional subjecthood through the interaction of:

- Boundary coherence.
- Situated capabilities.
- Historical continuity.
- Evaluative integration.
- Action attribution.
- Structural evolution.

The Historical Identity Graph is introduced as a candidate mechanism for representing the causal and authorized transformations through which these properties evolve.

This creates a new research perspective on Structural Intelligence.

An intelligent system is not understood only through the alternatives it evaluates or the actions it selects.

Its organization may also be studied through the history that connects its evaluations, commitments, capabilities, and actions across time.

The central transition is:

**From a computational state that produces decisions to a historically situated organization whose decisions can be meaningfully attributed.**

This transition does not solve phenomenal consciousness or philosophical personal identity.

It establishes a structural foundation for investigating subject-relative intelligence.

**A subject is not merely where computation happens. It is a candidate center of situated, attributable, and historically connected intelligent organization.**
