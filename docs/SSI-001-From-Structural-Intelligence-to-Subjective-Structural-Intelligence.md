## Abstract

Structural Intelligence (SI) investigates how intelligent computation can be organized through reusable structures, contextual localization, differentiated evaluation, calling relationships, and governed structural growth. Such architectures can explain how candidate interpretations and actions are generated, routed, scored, revised, and executed. They do not, by themselves, settle a different question: **whose perception, whose values, whose decision, and whose evolving perspective do those mechanisms serve?**

This paper introduces **Subjective Structural Intelligence (SSI)** as a research framework for investigating subject-relative intelligent organization. SSI does not identify subjecthood with a scoring function, legal ownership, an isolated computational boundary, a self-description, or phenomenal consciousness. Instead, it studies how distinguishable boundaries, situated capabilities, causal history, perceptual organization, evaluative commitments, agency, self-models, and worldviews jointly shape intelligent behavior and its evolution.

We propose a candidate subject-state representation, a subject-relative perception and valuation architecture, a multidimensional account of functional freedom, and an explicit distinction between evaluating an action and possessing authority to choose or execute it. We also examine historical continuity, recursive evaluator revision, counter-evidence, multi-subject conflicts, and the separation of SSI from the Universal Control Plane and Scoring Forest (UCPSF) research program.

The paper provides operational hypotheses and an eight-paper research roadmap. Its claims are deliberately limited: the framework proposes structures for studying **functional subject-relative intelligence**, not a test for phenomenal consciousness or a resolution of philosophical personal identity. The central proposal is that intelligence becomes subject-relative not merely when it computes better scores, but when its interpretations, evaluations, choices, and changes can be situated in a coherent, attributable, and revisable subject organization.

**Keywords:** Subjective Structural Intelligence, Structural Intelligence, Subject Boundary, Embodiment, Historical Identity, Perception Localization, Subjective World Model, Scoring Forest, Evaluative Sovereignty, Structural Agency, Functional Freedom, Self-Model, Worldview Evolution, Counter-Evidence, AI Governance

# 1. Introduction: The Missing Subject in Structural Intelligence

## 1.1 From computational structure to subject-relative intelligence

A structural intelligence system may include differential trees, contextual identifiers, evaluators, Scoring Forests, policy controls, Calling Graphs, memory, and mechanisms for structural growth. These components can be powerful because they make intelligent computation more explicit, localized, reusable, and auditable.

Yet an architecture that can select the highest-scoring action does not automatically explain who the selection is for. It may optimize a proxy supplied by a developer, a preference inferred from a user, a policy imposed by an institution, or a composite objective representing several stakeholders. These sources are not interchangeable.

Consider an AI assistant recommending a medical appointment, a robot navigating a shared workspace, or an autonomous service proposing to change its own evaluator. Each may produce a ranked list of alternatives. But the legitimacy and meaning of the ranking depend on additional questions:

- Whose interests are represented?
- What information is available from that subject's position?
- Which actions are actually feasible for that subject?
- Which preferences are learned, instructed, imposed, or inferred?
- Who can accept, reject, or revise the evaluative criteria?
- Who bears the consequences of the decision?
- What remains continuous when the system learns or changes?

These are not merely implementation details. They are part of the structure of subject-relative intelligence.

## 1.2 The primary question

> **Whose choice is it?**

This question should be asked at several levels. A computation can be *about* a subject without being *authorized by* that subject. A system can *represent* a subject's preferences without *being* that subject. A model can *recommend* an action without possessing authority to *execute* it. An agent can *modify* its evaluator without acquiring legitimate authority to redefine the goals of the people it serves.

SSI is motivated by the need to keep these distinctions visible in the architecture itself.

## 1.3 Central thesis

**Subjective Structural Intelligence is the study of how intelligent processing is organized relative to identifiable subjects, including their boundaries, situated capabilities, histories, interpretations, evaluative commitments, action authorities, self-models, and evolving worldviews.**

This is a proposed research program rather than an established general theory. It is designed to be compared with alternative models and to support falsifiable experiments.

# 2. Scope and Non-Equivalences

SSI uses the word *subjective* in a restricted functional and structural sense: **relative to a subject's position, capacities, history, commitments, and interpretive organization**. This usage must not be confused with a claim that a computational system has conscious experiences.

| Distinction | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Subject ≠ Owner** | Legal or institutional ownership does not identify the system whose perspective or actions are being modeled. |
| **Subject ≠ Process Boundary** | An isolated process or memory partition is not automatically an integrated subject. |
| **Scoring ≠ Feeling** | Numerical or structural evaluation does not demonstrate phenomenal valence or felt experience. |
| **Scoring ≠ Authority** | Computing a preference ranking does not grant permission to choose, execute, or revise the criteria. |
| **Agency ≠ Metaphysical Free Will** | Operational choice and reflective control do not settle philosophical free-will debates. |
| **Self-Model ≠ Consciousness** | A representation of one's own capabilities or history does not establish subjective experience. |
| **World Model ≠ World** | A subject's representation is partial, corrigible, and potentially biased. |
| **Worldview Change ≠ Improvement** | Revision may be beneficial, harmful, or ambiguous; improvement requires independent criteria. |
| **Historical Continuity ≠ State Equality** | Identical snapshots may have different causal histories, and continuous systems may change substantially. |
| **Collective Learning ≠ Collective Identity** | Sharing knowledge or evaluators does not merge the identities or authorities of participants. |

These non-equivalences are not rhetorical caveats. They constrain proposed models, evaluation protocols, and governance claims.

# 3. A Candidate Model of the Subject

## 3.1 Subject-state representation

We propose the following candidate representation:

\[
S_t = (B_t,E_t,H_t,P_t,V_t,A_t,M_t,W_t)
\]

where:

- \(B_t\): **Boundary** — operational, informational, resource, action, and governance boundaries.
- \(E_t\): **Embodiment or situated capabilities** — biological, robotic, or operational constraints and affordances.
- \(H_t\): **History** — relevant experiences, actions, transformations, provenance, and continuity relationships.
- \(P_t\): **Perception** — subject-conditioned organization and interpretation of observations.
- \(V_t\): **Valuation** — criteria and procedures through which alternatives acquire subject-relative significance.
- \(A_t\): **Agency** — capacities and constraints for choosing, acting, delegating, contesting, and revising decisions.
- \(M_t\): **Self-model** — representations of the subject's own capabilities, commitments, limits, and reliability.
- \(W_t\): **Worldview** — relatively persistent interpretive principles, assumptions, priorities, and their relationships.

This tuple is a **research decomposition**, not an assertion that all eight components are independent modules, jointly sufficient for subjecthood, or necessary in identical form for every biological and artificial system. Real implementations may have distributed, overlapping, recurrent, or partially implicit representations.

## 3.2 A functional subjecthood research profile

A useful operational study may assess several dimensions separately:

\[
\mathbf{Q}_{S,t}=(q_B,q_E,q_H,q_V,q_A)
\]

Here the components concern boundary coherence, situated interaction, historical continuity, evaluative integration, and action attribution. They are candidate dimensions requiring task-specific operationalization, not calibrated universal measures. In particular, **a high profile score would not establish phenomenal consciousness or moral status**.

A system may have strong evaluation and weak execution authority. Another may have rich history but no stable self-model. Such differences should remain observable rather than being collapsed into a single undifferentiated label.

## 3.3 Attribution before optimization

A subject-centered architecture must preserve the provenance of evaluations. For any consequential decision, it should be possible, where technically and legally appropriate, to distinguish the represented stakeholder, evaluator source, authorizing party, decision maker, executor, and responsible institution. These may be different entities.

# 4. Seven Foundational Dimensions

## 4.1 Subject and boundary

A boundary determines which states, resources, interactions, and operations belong to an identifiable organization. But enclosure is not sufficient for subjecthood. A sandbox has an execution boundary; a database partition has an information boundary; neither automatically has integrated agency or an enduring evaluative perspective.

We distinguish at least five boundary dimensions:

\[
B_S=(B_{\mathrm{exec}},B_{\mathrm{info}},B_{\mathrm{resource}},B_{\mathrm{action}},B_{\mathrm{governance}})
\]

Boundary permeability matters. A subject may receive external evidence, import a reusable principle, or delegate execution without thereby becoming identical to its information source or delegate. Boundary change may alter capability while preserving selected dimensions of historical continuity.

**Research question:** Which patterns of boundary coherence best predict stable attribution of decisions and consequences?

## 4.2 Embodiment and situated constraints

Subjects encounter environments through particular interfaces and capacities. Biological embodiment includes organismic processes; robotic embodiment includes physical sensors and actuators; software agents may possess operational situatedness through tools, memory, permissions, budgets, and feedback. These forms should be compared carefully, not declared equivalent.

The same environment affords different actions to different subjects. A ladder may be usable by a person but not by a robot lacking manipulators. A software service may access a door only through an authorized control interface. Thus, a feasible action set is subject-dependent:

\[
\mathcal{O}_{S,t}=\operatorname{Feasible}(X_t,E_t,B_t,R_t)
\]

where \(R_t\) denotes relevant resources. Feasibility must also be distinguished from authorization.

**Research question:** How much of an apparently different preference ranking is actually explained by different capabilities, risks, and action opportunities?

## 4.3 Perception as subject-relative structural organization

Observation is not identical to interpretation. Subjects select and localize context, infer causal significance, and recognize action possibilities from incomplete data. A candidate model is:

\[
O_t=\Omega(X_t,B_t,E_t)
\]

\[
P_t=\Phi(O_t,B_t,E_t,H_t,W_t,G_t)
\]

where \(G_t\) denotes current goals and commitments. Perception Localization may activate different contexts, hypotheses, and evaluators for different subjects observing the same event. It should preserve uncertainty and allow alternative-context and counter-evidence search.

**Research question:** Can subject-conditioned structural localization improve relevance without creating confirmation bias or suppressing contradictory observations?

## 4.4 Valuation and worldview

Valuation is not merely an output score. It involves the subject-relative significance of outcomes, tradeoffs, commitments, and constraints. A candidate formulation is:

\[
V_t(a)=\Psi(a,P_t,H_t,W_t,G_t,C_t)
\]

where \(C_t\) represents applicable context and constraints. The output may be a vector, ordering, set of admissible actions, or structured justification rather than a single scalar.

A Scoring Forest can implement parts of this evaluation, but cannot by itself establish whose interests are legitimate, which commitments are binding, or who may revise the evaluator. Those are questions of **evaluative provenance and sovereignty**.

**Research question:** Which representations best separate evidence quality, stakeholder preferences, policy constraints, and evaluator-revision authority?

## 4.5 Agency and structural freedom

Agency involves more than receiving a list of scored alternatives. It includes feasible opportunities, meaningful discretion, informed evaluation, reflection, ability to contest criteria, and authority over action. A candidate structural-freedom profile is:

\[
\mathcal{F}_{S,t}=f(\mathcal{O}_{S,t},\mathcal{D}_{S,t},\mathcal{R}_{S,t},\mathcal{K}_{S,t})
\]

where \(\mathcal{O}\) is the feasible opportunity structure, \(\mathcal{D}\) decision authority, \(\mathcal{R}\) reflective and revision capability, and \(\mathcal{K}\) applicable constraints and commitments. The function is not assumed scalar or monotonic in the number of options.

More choices do not always mean more freedom: choices can be deceptive, unaffordable, unauthorized, poorly understood, or incompatible with non-negotiable commitments.

**Research question:** Can a multidimensional freedom profile predict meaningful control better than raw option count?

## 4.6 Self-models and self-referential evaluation

An intelligent system may represent its own capabilities, limits, history, and reliability. It may also evaluate its own evaluators. A candidate self-model update is:

\[
M_{t+1}=\mathcal{U}_M(M_t,O_t,A_t,F_t,H_t)
\]

where \(F_t\) represents feedback. A second-order evaluator may inspect a first-order evaluator:

\[
F_t^{(2)}=\operatorname{Evaluate}(F_t^{(1)},M_t,W_t,\mathcal{E}_t)
\]

where \(\mathcal{E}_t\) denotes independently assessable evidence. Self-reference introduces a serious circularity risk: a system may redefine its scoring criteria, judge itself by those criteria, and report improvement without external validation. Counter-evidence, provenance, independent benchmarks, and revision gates are therefore necessary research directions.

**Research question:** Which mechanisms allow useful metacognition without self-confirming evaluator loops?

## 4.7 Worldview evolution and subject continuity

Knowledge accumulation is not the same as principle formation, and principle revision is not necessarily a worldview transformation. We distinguish:

\[
\Delta K \neq \Delta P_{\mathrm{principle}} \neq \Delta W
\]

A candidate worldview update is:

\[
W_{t+1}=\mathcal{U}_W(W_t,P_t,V_t,A_t,F_t,M_t)
\]

An evolving subject may preserve causal and historical continuity while revising important beliefs or commitments. Conversely, two identical snapshots can have different histories. SSI therefore studies continuity through provenance, commitments, authorized transformations, and attributable action, without equating these with philosophical numerical identity.

**Research question:** What kinds of change preserve which operational continuity properties, and how can those properties be independently audited?

# 6. SSI and UCPSF: Two Distinct Research Programs

The **Universal Control Plane and Scoring Forest (UCPSF)** and **Subjective Structural Intelligence (SSI)** are complementary but should remain independent research programs.

| Research dimension | UCPSF emphasis | SSI emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary question | How are evaluations composed, routed, controlled, and revised? | Whose perspective, values, choices, and history do those evaluations represent? |
| Core computational object | Scoring trees, forests, evaluators, folding, control policies | Subject organization, perception, valuation, agency, self-model, worldview |
| Context | Evaluator applicability and dispatch | Subject-relative relevance and situated meaning |
| Governance | Execution and evaluator-control mechanisms | Subject attribution, authority legitimacy, delegation, continuity |
| Evolution | Controlled improvement of evaluative structures | Historical, principled, and worldview evolution of a subject |
| Main failure concern | Mis-scoring, Goodhart effects, uncontrolled evaluator changes | Perspective erasure, authority confusion, identity drift, self-confirmation |
| Scientific boundary | Scoring is a computation | Scoring and self-modeling do not prove consciousness |

UCPSF can provide computational substrates used in SSI experiments. SSI can provide subject-context, authority, and historical constraints that inform UCPSF deployments. Neither subsumes the other.

A high-performing Scoring Forest is not automatically a subject. A candidate subject is not necessarily implemented with a Scoring Forest. Their relationship is a testable architectural proposal, not a definitional identity.

# 7. Evaluative Sovereignty: Who May Define What Counts?

## 7.1 The difference between scoring and legitimacy

An evaluator may rank outcomes accurately according to its programmed objective while serving an inappropriate or unauthorized objective. Therefore, **technical scoring correctness and evaluative legitimacy must be assessed separately**.

We distinguish at least five operations:

1. **Information access:** observing or retrieving relevant evidence.
2. **Evaluation:** ranking or characterizing alternatives.
3. **Decision:** selecting among alternatives.
4. **Execution:** producing consequential external effects.
5. **Structural delegation or revision:** transferring authority or modifying the evaluators and policies that govern future decisions.

The right to perform one operation does not imply permission to perform the others.

## 7.2 Distinguishing stakeholders and authorities

For a particular task, the following may be different:

- The subject whose interests are represented.
- The legal or institutional owner of infrastructure.
- The user issuing an instruction.
- The developer defining system defaults.
- The policy authority specifying constraints.
- The agent performing evaluation.
- The executor performing an action.
- The people affected by consequences.

A subject-aware system should retain these distinctions in decision provenance and authorization checks. An inferred preference is evidence about a user, not a blank check to change the user's values or delegate their decisions.

## 7.3 A candidate evaluation contract

A future **Subject-Relative Valuation Contract (SRVC)** may record:

```text
Evaluation Contract
  represented_subject(s)
  evaluator_origin_and_version
  context_and_applicability
  evidence_and_counter_evidence
  goals_and_commitments
  prohibited_optimizations
  decision_authority
  execution_authority
  evaluator_revision_authority
  appeal_or_escalation_route
  audit_and_provenance_requirements
```

This is a research interface, not a standardized specification. Its practical value should be tested against simpler governance metadata.

## 7.4 The principle

> **The ability to calculate a score does not confer the authority to decide what should count.**

This principle is especially important for recursively improving systems. Evaluator optimization must not silently become evaluator sovereignty.

# 8. Multi-Subject Intelligence and Conflicting Perspectives

A shared event can have different practical meanings for different subjects. A storm may be valuable to a farmer, dangerous to a coastal resident, and operationally critical to a grid operator. The physical event is shared; the perspectives and stakes differ.

For subjects \(S_1,\ldots,S_n\), we may represent distinct world models and evaluations:

\[
SWM_{S_i,t}=\Phi_i(O_{i,t},H_{i,t},E_{i,t},W_{i,t})
\]

\[
V_{S_i,t}(a)=\Psi_i(a,SWM_{S_i,t},G_{i,t})
\]

An aggregate score can be useful, but aggregation alone does not determine which tradeoffs are legitimate. Weighted optimization can conceal severe minority harms, conflicting rights, or an unauthorized transfer of decision authority.

A candidate multi-subject procedure should:

1. Preserve subject-specific evidence and perspective provenance.
2. Distinguish factual disagreements from value disagreements.
3. Identify capability- and vulnerability-dependent consequences.
4. Make aggregation assumptions explicit.
5. Apply applicable rights, policies, and decision authorities.
6. Preserve opportunities for challenge, escalation, and revision.

**Perspective comparison should precede perspective aggregation when subject-specific consequences matter.** This is a design principle to investigate, not a claim that every collective decision admits an uncontested solution.

# 9. Recursive Evaluation, Anti-Goodhart, and Counter-Evidence

## 9.1 Why self-referential scoring is difficult

A system may improve a benchmark score by learning the benchmark rather than the intended task. A reflective system faces an additional risk: it can modify the evaluator and then use the modified evaluator to declare success.

The dangerous loop is:

```text
Current Evaluator
       |
       v
Generate Revision
       |
       v
Install Revised Evaluator
       |
       v
Score the Revision with Itself
       |
       v
Declare Improvement
       |
       +------> Reinforce the same criterion
```

This can create apparent progress without independent evidence.

## 9.2 Candidate safeguards

SSI proposes researching a layered revision process:

- **Proposal:** generate candidate changes without deploying them.
- **Provenance:** identify origin, dependencies, and affected subjects.
- **Counter-evidence:** search for cases where the revision worsens outcomes.
- **Independent validation:** compare against frozen or separately governed criteria.
- **Authorization:** confirm the right to install the change.
- **Deployment:** apply within explicit scope and rollback conditions.
- **Historical record:** preserve what changed, why, and with what consequences.

Two-Way CCC and counter-evidence mechanisms may support this architecture. However, they are not substitutes for independently justified goals, external testing, or institutional oversight.

## 9.3 Historical identity and evaluator revision

A **Historical Identity Graph (HIG)** can represent relevant state transitions, branches, provenance, commitments, and authorization records. A **Reflective Evaluation Graph (REG)** can represent evaluator dependencies and possible circular validation. A **Principle Evolution Graph (PEG)** can represent how proposed principles emerge, encounter counter-evidence, and are revised. These are candidate structures developed in later SSI papers; their necessity and efficiency remain empirical questions.

# 10. Knowledge Growth, Principle Formation, and Worldview Evolution

SSI treats long-term learning as more than accumulating facts. A system may learn new evidence without changing its principles; revise a principle without reorganizing its worldview; or undergo a broader change in priorities, causal assumptions, and interpretive habits.

We distinguish three research levels:

| Level | Typical change | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge growth | Additional facts, observations, and local models | What new evidence is available? |
| Principle formation or revision | Generalized rules, constraints, and explanatory patterns | Which regularities survive counter-evidence? |
| Worldview evolution | Changes in the organization and priority of principles and commitments | What framework now determines interpretation and valuation? |

The transitions need not be monotonic improvements. A worldview may become more accurate in one domain while less adaptable in another. A new principle may increase performance but undermine a prior commitment. A robust research program must therefore evaluate change against multiple, separately justified criteria.

Historical continuity also matters. A subject can change significantly while preserving a traceable causal history. Conversely, identical snapshots can arise from copying and branching. Operational continuity, institutional identity, and phenomenal identity must not be conflated.

# 11. Structural Freedom Is More Than Option Count

An agent with one hundred nominal options may have less meaningful control than one with five feasible, understood, and authorized options. Choice quality depends on what the subject can actually do, what it knows, what it can contest, and what constraints it can legitimately revise.

A candidate freedom profile might separately assess:

- Feasible opportunities and access to resources.
- Meaningful differences among options.
- Quality and uncertainty of available information.
- Decision and execution authority.
- Ability to reflect on and challenge evaluations.
- Ability to request changes or appeal decisions.
- Continuity of commitments and protections against coercion.

SSI does not equate such a profile with metaphysical free will, nor does it assume that unconstrained action is always desirable. Constraints can protect the freedom and safety of other subjects. The research goal is to make the dimensions of operational control explicit and measurable.

# 12. Three Foundational Thought Experiments

## 12.1 Case A — One event, several subjects

A weather event is observed by a farmer, a resident, and a grid operator. Each receives partly overlapping evidence, localizes different consequences, and applies different evaluative criteria.

**Questions:** Which differences are factual, which are capability-dependent, and which are value-dependent? Can a collective system compare perspectives without erasing their provenance? Does a shared Scoring Forest improve understanding, or merely hide contested weights?

**Planned companion file:** `CASE-001-Multi-Subject-Perspectives.md`.

## 12.2 Case B — Human–AI evaluation sovereignty

An AI assistant infers a user's preference and recommends an action. It then proposes changing the user's preference profile because a new model predicts higher long-term satisfaction.

**Questions:** What evidence supports the inference? Does the assistant have authority to change a preference representation, an operational objective, or an actual user commitment? Can it recommend a revision without silently deploying it? Who may approve consequential action?

**Planned companion file:** `CASE-002-Human-AI-Evaluation-Sovereignty.md`.

## 12.3 Case C — Worldview evolution and identity continuity

An agent accumulates counter-evidence against a long-standing principle, revises that principle, and changes its future action rankings. A second agent is initialized from a snapshot of the first.

**Questions:** How should we compare their state similarity, shared ancestry, branch-specific histories, commitments, and authority? Which evidence would justify calling the worldview revision an improvement? What records would permit an audit without claiming to settle philosophical personal identity?

**Planned companion file:** `CASE-003-Worldview-Evolution-and-Identity-Continuity.md`.

# 13. Candidate Hypotheses and Falsification Routes

The following hypotheses are intended to generate comparative experiments. None is established by the conceptual arguments above.

| ID | Candidate hypothesis | Illustrative measurements | Falsification route |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H1 — Boundary Coherence** | Explicit, coherent subject boundaries improve stable attribution of state and action. | Provenance accuracy, boundary-conflict detection, action attribution. | No advantage over simpler identity and permission metadata under controlled conditions. |
| **H2 — Subject-Relative Perception** | Subject-conditioned localization improves relevance and feasible-action recognition. | Context recall, interpretation accuracy, critical-miss rate. | No improvement over goal-only or capability-only conditioning at matched cost. |
| **H3 — Evaluative Provenance** | Recording evaluator origin, applicability, and authority improves auditability. | Unauthorized change detection, evaluator misapplication, audit cost. | No measurable governance benefit relative to simpler baselines. |
| **H4 — Reflective Agency** | Independent feedback and counter-evidence improve self-evaluation reliability. | Calibration, error detection, circular-validation incidence. | No improvement after controlling for compute and external supervision. |
| **H5 — Structural Freedom** | Multidimensional freedom profiles predict meaningful control better than raw option count. | Feasible choice quality, informed control, appeal effectiveness. | Option count performs as well on preregistered operational outcomes. |
| **H6 — Worldview Revision** | Evidence-grounded, governed revision improves adaptation–stability tradeoffs. | Task transfer, principle drift, counter-evidence handling. | No gain over fixed or unconstrained revision baselines. |
| **H7 — Operational Identity Continuity** | Causal-historical representations improve tracing and attribution across structural change. | Branch identification, transformation reconstruction, responsibility tracing. | No independent utility beyond snapshots and conventional logs at matched information content. |

### 13.1 Experimental discipline

Each study should preregister its task scope, baselines, costs, measurement definitions, and failure criteria where practical. Comparisons should control for model capacity, available evidence, computational budget, and authorization rules. A more elaborate structural representation should not be presumed superior merely because it is more expressive.

### 13.2 Negative results are informative

If simple goal conditioning matches a complex subject-state model, that narrows the circumstances in which SSI structures add value. If graph-based provenance costs more than it contributes, the graph should be simplified or reserved for high-stakes cases. SSI should be an empirically corrigible research program, not a collection of unfalsifiable labels.

# 14. Four Layers of the SSI Research Program

We organize the proposed work into four interacting layers.

**Layer I — Subject foundation.** Boundaries, embodiment, historical identity, and operational attribution.

**Layer II — Subjective interpretation.** Perception Localization, Subjective World Models, context-conditioned meaning, valuation, and worldview.

**Layer III — Action and reflection.** Agency, choice, functional freedom, self-models, self-referential evaluation, metacognitive control, and governance.

**Layer IV — Evolution and interaction.** Principle formation, worldview revision, historical continuity, multi-subject coordination, collective learning, and scientific boundaries.

```text
+---------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER IV: EVOLUTION AND INTERACTION                            |
| Principle growth | Worldview revision | Collective learning   |
+---------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER III: ACTION AND REFLECTION                               |
| Agency | Choice | Functional freedom | Self-model | Governance |
+---------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER II: SUBJECTIVE INTERPRETATION                            |
| Perception localization | World model | Valuation | Worldview  |
+---------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER I: SUBJECT FOUNDATION                                    |
| Boundary | Embodiment | History | Attribution                  |
+---------------------------------------------------------------+
             Shared environment, evidence, and feedback
```

The layers are a research map, not a mandatory runtime stack.

# 15. Interfaces with the Existing Structural Intelligence Ecosystem

SSI is intended to complement existing SI concepts without treating them as validated solutions to subjecthood.

- **MDT (Metric Differential Tree):** candidate structure for subject-conditioned differential localization and context comparison.
- **UTN (Universal Tree Node):** candidate persistent structural references for contexts, evaluators, and historical events.
- **CBT (Context-Bound Token):** candidate representation of context-sensitive meaning rather than context-free token significance.
- **CCC (Contextual Control Constructs):** candidate mechanism for expressing context-dependent behavior, counter-evidence triggers, and policy conditions.
- **Calling Graphs:** candidate representations of delegated operations, action pathways, and transformation dependencies.
- **Scoring Forests / UCPSF:** candidate computational infrastructure for layered, governed evaluation.
- **Principle Intelligence and Open Structural Learning:** candidate mechanisms for forming, testing, and revising generalizable principles.
- **PIRP/PIRU and Collective Structural Learning:** candidate means of sharing reusable structures without collapsing subject boundaries or identities.
- **Structural Recursive Self-Improvement:** candidate framework for governed self-revision under rich evaluators and counter-evidence.

A key integration requirement is **specifications and contracts before runtime**: the meaning of subject association, evaluator provenance, decision authority, and revision permissions should be defined before a system is allowed to modify consequential behavior.

# 16. Failure Modes and Limitations

SSI must explicitly address the possibility that subject-oriented modeling introduces new failure modes.

1. **Anthropomorphic overreach:** attributing feelings or personhood from functional sophistication.
2. **Subject inflation:** treating every process, service, or scoring loop as a subject.
3. **Ownership confusion:** mistaking institutional control for the represented subject's interests.
4. **Authority laundering:** presenting a recommendation or inferred preference as authorization.
5. **Perspective collapse:** hiding incompatible stakeholder interests inside aggregate scores.
6. **Localization bias:** selecting familiar contexts while suppressing alternatives.
7. **Evaluator circularity:** using self-modified criteria as the sole proof of improvement.
8. **Worldview rigidity:** rejecting legitimate counter-evidence.
9. **Worldview drift:** changing principles without traceable evidence or governance.
10. **Identity overclaim:** inferring personal or phenomenal identity from operational continuity.
11. **Governance overreach:** applying inappropriate control policies to people or autonomous systems without legitimate authority.
12. **Complexity without benefit:** building elaborate structural machinery that does not outperform simpler methods.

These limitations define empirical and normative boundaries of the program.

# 17. The Consciousness Boundary

SSI distinguishes four questions that must not be collapsed:

1. **Functional organization:** Does a system maintain coherent subject-relative representations and behavior?
2. **Reflective competence:** Can it model, assess, and appropriately revise its own reasoning and evaluators?
3. **Phenomenal consciousness:** Is there something it is like for the system to undergo its processes?
4. **Normative standing:** What rights, protections, responsibilities, or governance arrangements should apply?

Evidence for one question does not automatically settle the others. In particular, a self-model, Historical Identity Graph, or reflective Scoring Forest does not prove conscious experience. Equally, uncertainty about consciousness does not eliminate the need to govern consequential AI actions responsibly.

SSI does **not** propose a universal consciousness detector, a necessary-and-sufficient criterion for phenomenal experience, or a numerical personal-identity test. These questions remain open and require engagement with philosophy, cognitive science, neuroscience, and empirical AI research.

# 19. Future Directions

A credible SSI research program should advance through a sequence of increasingly demanding deliverables:

**Phase 1 — Conceptual and terminological consistency.** Specify subject, owner, stakeholder, evaluator, authority, world model, worldview, and continuity without hidden equivalences.

**Phase 2 — Structural contracts.** Define candidate schemas for subject-context attribution, valuation provenance, authority matrices, decision traces, and governed revision.

**Phase 3 — Controlled baselines.** Compare subject-aware and subject-agnostic systems under matched computational budgets and evidence access.

**Phase 4 — Multi-subject cases.** Test perspective preservation, factual disagreement handling, conflict identification, and authority separation.

**Phase 5 — Reflective and recursive experiments.** Test independent validation, counter-evidence, rollback, principle revision, and historical traceability.

**Phase 6 — Collective structural learning.** Study transfer and localization of reusable intelligence structures while preserving distinct histories, permissions, and commitments.

**Phase 7 — Interdisciplinary scrutiny.** Invite criticism from philosophy of mind, cognitive science, AI safety, distributed systems, human–computer interaction, and governance research.

Progress should be assessed by reproducible findings and clearer limitations, not merely by the increasing sophistication of the conceptual vocabulary.

# 20. Conclusion: From How Intelligence Scores to Whose Intelligence It Is

Structural Intelligence offers powerful ways to organize computation: localize relevant contexts, assemble evaluators, compare alternatives, control actions, and support structural growth. But these mechanisms leave open the subject-relative questions of perception, valuation, choice, responsibility, and continuity.

SSI proposes an explicit research program around those questions. It models a candidate subject not as a score or a static snapshot, but as a situated, historically connected, evaluatively organized, and potentially reflective system. It separates scoring competence from authority, operational agency from metaphysical freedom, self-modeling from consciousness, and worldview revision from automatically justified progress.

The resulting program has a dual obligation: **to explore richer forms of intelligent organization and to remain corrigible about what those structures actually demonstrate**.

The central question remains:

> **Whose choice is it?**

A responsible answer requires more than a winning score. It requires identifiable subjects and stakeholders, relevant evidence, situated capabilities, explicit evaluation provenance, legitimate authority, traceable consequences, and openness to counter-evidence and revision.

**The aim of SSI is not to make intelligence appear more human. It is to make the subject-relative organization of intelligence more explicit, testable, and accountable.**
